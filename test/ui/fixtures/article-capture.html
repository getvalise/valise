The engineer who measured Wrenmouth harbour

By Mara Lindqvist and Tomas Oyelaran · 14 September 2026

The harbour at Wrenmouth was dredged for the first time in 1851, and the record of that work survives in a ledger kept by the engineer Ada Kettering. Her entries are short, practical and dated, and they show a town learning to measure the sea floor before it tried to change it.

Kettering began with soundings. Every morning she rowed out with two apprentices, dropped a weighted line at marked intervals and wrote the depth beside a pencilled grid. After six weeks the grid showed what the fishermen already suspected: a bar of silt had grown across the mouth of the harbour, and at low water the channel was barely deep enough for a laden boat.

The town council wanted a quick answer. Kettering gave them a slow one. She argued that dredging without understanding the current would only move the silt from one place to another, and she spent the following winter tracking floats released at different states of the tide. The floats drifted east along the shore, then turned back into the harbour on the flood.

Her conclusion was that the bar was fed by the town's own breakwater, built forty years earlier, which slowed the current just enough for silt to settle. The council did not like the conclusion, because the breakwater had been expensive and was considered a success. Kettering wrote in the ledger that a structure can succeed at the job it was built for and still cause a problem nobody asked it to solve.

The compromise was a cut through the end of the breakwater, narrow enough to keep the swell out and wide enough to let the current scour the channel. It took two summers to build. By 1856 the soundings showed the bar shrinking without any further dredging, and the ledger entries become shorter and less frequent, as if there were less to say.

What makes the ledger worth reading today is not the engineering but the method. Kettering measured before she acted, wrote down what she expected to see, and recorded when she was wrong. Twice she notes that a float went the opposite way from her prediction, and both times she changes the plan rather than the data.

The ledger now sits in the county archive, where it is catalogued under the harbour board rather than under her name. Anyone who wants to understand how small towns once managed their coastlines could do worse than start with her grid of pencilled numbers and the patient notes beside them.
